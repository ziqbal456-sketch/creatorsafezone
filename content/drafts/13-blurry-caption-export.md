# BLURRY CAPTIONS After Export? Check These Steps

Find the first stage where the captions become soft: the editor preview, the saved video or the uploaded playback. Comparing those stages is more useful than repeatedly increasing every quality setting. Keep the original project and make a short test export containing the affected text.

This guide concerns visible detail in the rendered words. If the words are sharp but difficult to read over the footage, use the separate [caption readability guide](https://creatorsafezone.com/guides/video-caption-size-contrast-readability).

## Compare at a Useful Viewing Size

Open the saved export in a local player and pause on the same caption you inspected in the editor. Check at normal viewing size and, when investigating pixels, at a one-to-one display scale. Filling a much larger monitor with a smaller video enlarges its pixels; that alone does not identify a faulty export.

Also inspect the editor's preview-quality setting. A reduced-resolution preview can look soft even when the final render is acceptable. Judge the actual exported file before changing a caption style that already works.

## Separate Text Problems from Whole-Frame Problems

If the footage and captions both lose detail, compare the project's dimensions with the export dimensions. Check whether you accidentally exported a smaller version or reused a compressed copy. CapCut's [official blurry-video troubleshooting](https://www.capcut.com/help/fix-blurry-video) identifies source quality and export settings as relevant checks; its controls are specific to that editor.

If only the captions are soft, identify whether they are editable text or imported graphics. A small caption image enlarged inside the editor has limited source detail. Recreate it at an appropriate size or return to editable text rather than exporting the same small image at a higher video resolution.

For an illustrative case, stretching a 540-pixel-wide full-frame graphic to 1080 pixels doubles its width. The larger output cannot recover letter detail missing from that source. This calculation describes scaling, not a universal caption-size recommendation.

## Change One Export Variable at a Time

Keep the same short section and compare two exports. Start with the intended dimensions and an appropriate quality preset for your editor. If you investigate compression, hold dimensions and caption styling steady so you can see what changed.

Avoid cycling through social downloads, messaging-app copies and fresh exports as if they were equivalent masters. Name the tests clearly and compare them with the original project. A sharpening effect may alter edges, but it cannot reconstruct detail lost in a tiny source graphic or a severely reduced export.

## Check Uploaded Playback Separately

If the local file is clear and the uploaded version is soft, check the destination's available playback quality and whether processing has finished. Compare the same timestamp. Do not assume that a single low-quality preview proves the file itself is defective, or that a particular bitrate will prevent every platform from processing it.

Keep a record of which export you uploaded. Otherwise, it is easy to assess an earlier test while changing the newest project.

## Finish with a Placement Review

After resolving the rendering problem, inspect the captions at phone size over moving footage. Load the final captioned export into the [comparison checker](https://creatorsafezone.com/check-all-platforms) to review the marked text block against its planning regions. The checker does not measure sharpness or improve video quality. It helps catch the separate problem of a clear caption sitting beneath interface controls.
