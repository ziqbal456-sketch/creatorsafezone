# TRANSLATED SUBTITLES Too Long? Fix the Layout

A translation can need different line breaks, more screen space or more reading time than the original caption. Review each language as its own timed layout. Reusing the source caption's rectangle and making the translated font smaller can leave the words technically visible but difficult to follow.

Start with a corrected source transcript and the final video cut. Keep separate, clearly named subtitle files or project versions for each language so an edit to one does not silently overwrite another.

## Check Meaning Before Fitting the Box

Have a fluent reviewer check the translation in context, including names, references, negation and speaker changes. Automatic translation can provide a draft, but fitting that draft on screen does not establish that it conveys the right message.

If the words must be shortened, preserve the meaning and the important audio information. Do not remove a qualifying word or speaker label simply to recover a few pixels. Record approved terminology when a series repeats the same product, person or technical phrase.

## Rebuild Line Breaks for the Target Language

Read the complete sentence before dividing it. Keep meaningful word groups together where the target language allows it, and review the actual rendered result rather than only a character counter.

[Netflix's general timed-text guide](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/215758617-Timed-Text-Style-Guide-General-Requirements) provides examples of grammatical units to preserve across line breaks. Those are Netflix delivery requirements and editorial examples, not universal social-platform dimensions. A character limit from one language or delivery specification does not define how every font and script will occupy a portrait frame.

For a hypothetical English example, “Keep the original file / before making changes” preserves two useful phrases. Breaking after “the” would separate it from “original file.” Apply the equivalent grammatical judgment in the translated language rather than mechanically copying the English break.

## Balance Space and Reading Time

If the translation becomes too dense, first review the wording and line breaks. Consider separate sequential cues where the speech and available timing permit them. Splitting one crowded cue into two very brief flashes does not automatically make it readable.

Play the result at normal speed with the picture. Check whether a viewer can read the caption while following the action, especially around cuts or demonstrations. If you change cue times, recheck synchronization against the final audio and use the [subtitle timing guide](https://creatorsafezone.com/guides/subtitles-out-of-sync) when the problem is an offset or drift.

## Inspect the Script in the Actual Renderer

Confirm that your selected font includes the characters you need. Look for missing-glyph boxes, clipped accent marks, unexpected word joining and punctuation in the wrong visual position. Right-to-left text mixed with numbers or Latin names needs review in the editor and final viewing environment; a plain-text document alone cannot establish the result.

If subtitles are delivered as a separate track, the destination may control their appearance. If they are burned into the picture, check the exported video. Keep this delivery choice explicit when handing files to another editor.

## Review Each Language's Placement

Export a captioned sample for each version and load it into the [comparison checker](https://creatorsafezone.com/check-all-platforms). Mark the entire translated block, including its background. A source-language layout that fit previously does not establish that the translated block fits too.

CreatorSafeZone does not translate or import subtitle files. Use it for the visual placement step after language and timing review, then confirm the intended destination preview. Keep the approved translation, timing file and matching video revision together for future corrections.
